### **1. Descriptive Data Analysis**
*   **Statistical Analysis:** The process of converting raw data into useful information or insights by collecting, summarizing, and interpreting it.
*   **Data Representation:** Creating a numerical format for data (like text or audio) so it can be manipulated by computers.
*   **Tokenization/Word Embedding:** Representing words as numbers or vectors so they can be analyzed by machines.
*   **Spectrogram:** A visual way to represent an audio signal's frequencies over time.
*   **Narrative:** The "storyline" in data storytelling that helps people understand what happened, why it happened, and what should be done next.
*   **Simpson Paradox:** A situation where a total summary number gives the opposite conclusion of its individual sub-groups.
*   **Population:** The entire group that a researcher is interested in studying.
*   **Sample:** A smaller subgroup of the population that is actually examined to collect data.
*   **Producing Data:** The first step of statistical analysis, which involves choosing a sample and collecting information from it.
*   **Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA):** The process of summarizing and exploring collected data to understand its basic features.
*   **Probability:** A mathematical way to understand how much a sample might differ from the true population.
*   **Inference:** Drawing final conclusions about a whole population based on the discoveries made from a sample.
*   **Observational Study:** A study where the researcher observes a group without interfering or changing anything.
*   **Experimental Study:** A study where the researcher actively changes variables to see if there is a cause-and-effect relationship.
*   **Descriptive Study:** A study that simply summarizes data to answer "what is happening?".
*   **Survey:** Data collected through interviews or questionnaires to learn about opinions or behaviors.
*   **Case Study:** An in-depth, detailed analysis of just one individual or a specific group.
*   **Variable:** A specific characteristic or feature (like age or height) of an individual in a dataset.
*   **Qualitative (Categorical):** Data that describes qualities. This includes **Nominal** (no specific order, like hair color) and **Ordinal** (has a ranking, like grades).
*   **Quantitative (Numerical):** Data that represents numbers. This includes **Discrete** (whole counts, like number of students) and **Continuous** (measurements that can have decimals, like weight).
*   **Structured Data:** Highly organized data that follows a fixed format, like a table in a database.
*   **Unstructured Data:** Data with no predefined format, such as videos, emails, or social media posts.
*   **Semi-Structured Data:** Data that doesn't fit a standard table but uses markers or tags (like JSON files) to separate elements.
*   **Metadata:** "Data about the data," or extra context that describes and organizes the main values (like GPS location on a photo).
*   **Histogram:** A chart that shows the distribution of a numeric variable using bars.
*   **Bin:** The range of numeric values covered by a single bar in a histogram.
*   **Mean:** The average value of a dataset.
*   **Median:** The middle value when data is listed in order.
*   **Mode:** The most frequent value or response in a variable.
*   **Standard Deviation:** A measure of the typical distance of each data point from the average.
*   **Variance:** A measure of variability in squared units.
*   **Coefficient of Variation (CV):** A percentage that shows how much data varies relative to its mean; used to compare consistency across different scales.
*   **Percentile:** A value below which a certain percentage of the data falls (e.g., the 85th percentile is higher than 85% of others).
*   **Five-Number Summary:** Five values (Min, Q1, Median, Q3, Max) that quickly describe the center and spread of data.
*   **Box-and-Whisker Plot:** A graph of the five-number summary used to see symmetry and identify outliers.
*   **Interquartile Range (IQR):** The distance between the 25th and 75th percentiles (Q1 to Q3).
*   **Outlier:** A data point that is extremely different from the rest of the dataset.
*   **Kurtosis:** A measure of how much data is in the center versus the "tails" or extremes of a distribution.
*   **Skewness:** A measure of how asymmetric a distribution is. **Right-skewed** data has a long tail on the right; **Left-skewed** has a long tail on the left.
*   **Scatter Plot:** A chart of dots used to show the relationship between two different variables.
*   **Time Chart:** A graph showing how a variable changes and evolves over a period of time.

### **2. Core Probability Terms**
*   **Probability:** A measure of how likely an event is to happen, represented as a number between **0 (impossible)** and **1 (certain)**.
*   **Experiment:** Any process used to collect observations or measurements, like tossing a die.
*   **Event:** A specific outcome or result of an experiment.
*   **Simple Event:** The most basic outcome of an experiment that cannot be broken down any further.
*   **Sample Space (S):** The set containing all possible outcomes of an experiment.
*   **Union ($A \cup B$):** An event where either $A$ occurs, $B$ occurs, or both occur.
*   **Intersection ($A \cap B$):** An event where both $A$ and $B$ occur at the same time.
*   **Complement ($A^C$):** All possible outcomes in the sample space that are **not** part of event $A$.
*   **Mutually Exclusive:** Two events that cannot happen at the same time; if one occurs, the other cannot.
*   **Independent Events:** Two events where the occurrence of one does not change the probability of the other.
*   **Conditional Probability ($P(A|B)$):** The likelihood of event $A$ happening **given** that event $B$ has already happened.

### **3. Probability Distributions**
*   **Random Variable (RV):** A mathematical quantity that represents the different possible outcomes of a random event.
*   **Discrete RV:** A variable with countable, finite possible values (e.g., number of software faults).
*   **Continuous RV:** A variable with infinite possible values along an interval (e.g., CPU temperature).
*   **Probability Distribution:** A "shape" or model that shows how probabilities are spread across all possible outcomes of a random variable.
*   **Population:** The entire group or theoretical behavior of a variable across all possible outcomes.
*   **Sample:** A small subset of observations taken from the population to estimate its characteristics.
*   **Mean ($\mu$):** The theoretical center or average value of a population.
*   **Variance ($\sigma^2$):** A measure of how spread out the values are in a population.
*   **Standard Deviation ($\sigma$):** The square root of the variance; a common measure of data spread.
*   **Probability Mass Function (PMF):** A function that assigns a specific probability to each value of a **discrete** random variable.
*   **Probability Density Function (PDF):** A smooth curve that describes the distribution of a **continuous** variable; probabilities are areas under this curve.
*   **Binomial Distribution:** A discrete distribution counting "successes" in a fixed number of independent trials.
*   **Normal Distribution:** A symmetric, bell-shaped curve where the mean, median, and mode are all equal.
*   **Z-score:** The number of standard deviations a specific value sits away from the mean.
*   **Sampling Distribution:** The distribution formed by a statistic (like a mean) when you take many repeated samples.
*   **Central Limit Theorem (CLT):** The rule stating that the distribution of sample means becomes **Normal** as the sample size increases, regardless of the original data's shape.

### **4. Statistical Inference and Estimation**
*   **Statistical Inference:** The process of drawing conclusions about a whole population based on a smaller sample.
*   **Estimation:** Finding the most likely values for unknown population parameters (like the mean or proportion).
*   **Estimator:** A specific formula or rule used to calculate an estimate from sample data.
*   **Unbiased Estimator:** A formula that, on average, accurately targets the true population parameter without systematic error.
*   **Standard Error (SE):** The standard deviation of a sampling distribution; it measures the "spread" of our estimates.
*   **Confidence Interval (CI):** A range of values around a sample estimate that is likely to contain the true population parameter.
*   **Margin of Error (MOE):** The range of uncertainty surrounding an estimate; it shows how far the estimate might be from the truth.
*   **Confidence Level ($1-\alpha$):** The percentage of the time the interval would correctly contain the parameter if you took many samples (usually 95% or 99%).
*   **Critical Value ($c_{\alpha/2}$):** A value (like $Z$ or $t$) used to scale the standard error to reach a specific confidence level.
*   **t-distribution:** A bell-shaped distribution with "heavier tails" used when the population spread is unknown or the sample size is small.
*   **Degrees of Freedom (df):** A parameter for the t-distribution, usually calculated as the sample size minus one ($n-1$).

### **5. Hypothesis Testing**
*   **Null Hypothesis ($H_0$):** The "boring" status quo or statement we assume is true until we find evidence against it.
*   **Alternative Hypothesis ($H_a$):** The "exciting" new idea or statement we want to prove is true.
*   **Test Statistic:** A value calculated from sample data used to decide whether to reject the null hypothesis.
*   **p-value:** The probability of getting your observed result if the null hypothesis were actually true; smaller p-values provide stronger evidence for rejecting the null.
*   **Significance Level ($\alpha$):** The maximum risk of making a mistake that you are willing to accept (usually 0.05).
*   **Type I Error:** Rejecting the null hypothesis when it was actually true (a "false positive").
*   **Type II Error:** Failing to reject the null hypothesis when it was actually false (a "false negative").
*   **One-tailed Test:** A test looking for a change in only one specific direction (e.g., "is the mean greater than 10?").
*   **Two-tailed Test:** A test looking for any change in either direction (e.g., "is the mean different from 10?").
